Question: How is the progress with for different SDGs? Will we be able to meet the targets? How does the progress varies across different regions and why?

Answer: Progress across the 17 SDGs is real but far too slow; on current trends the world will not meet most 2030 targets. Only about 18% of SDG targets are on track, nearly half are progressing too slowly, and close to a fifth are regressing. Extreme poverty has fallen only modestly since 2015 and has effectively stalled at around 6.9% of the world’s population (about 800 million people); eradicating extreme poverty by 2030 is now considered unlikely. Hunger affects about 1 in 11 people, with over 2 billion experiencing moderate or severe food insecurity. There has been progress in maternal and child mortality, school completion, electrification (from 84% to about 92% since 2015), and internet use (from 40% to 68%), but gains are uneven and slowing. Environmental and climate goals show the largest gap: temperatures have exceeded the 1.5°C threshold in recent years, and many indicators for SDGs 13–15 are regressing.

Progress varies sharply by region. High-income regions perform well on poverty, health, and education but poorly on responsible consumption, climate, and biodiversity. Sub-Saharan Africa concentrates most of the world’s extreme poor and out-of-school children, has low electrification and high maternal and child mortality, and is highly vulnerable to climate; progress is constrained by fiscal space, debt, conflict, and weak infrastructure. South Asia and parts of Latin America show gains in some social indicators but struggle with inequality, informal work, and climate shocks. Small island developing states face existential climate and disaster risks that reverse development gains. We will not meet most SDG targets by 2030 without unprecedented acceleration, especially in low- and lower-middle-income countries.


Question: How does the Pandemic (Covid-19) has affected the progress in SDGs?

Answer: The COVID-19 pandemic significantly set back SDG progress. It caused the first rise in global extreme poverty in decades and increased food insecurity; poverty reduction has not returned to pre-2015 momentum, and food prices remain well above pre-pandemic levels in many countries. Healthy life expectancy, which had improved for years, fell by about 1.8 years; health systems were overwhelmed and routine services (including childhood immunization) were disrupted, with immunization coverage still not fully restored. School closures affected over a billion learners, and in low-income countries limited digital access led to learning loss and higher dropout; today about 36% of school-age children in low-income countries are out of school versus 3% in high-income countries. Women were disproportionately hit by job losses in informal sectors and increased unpaid care, and inequalities in digital access, finance, and social protection widened. Governments took on large debt to fund health and social responses; many developing countries now face a multi-trillion-dollar annual SDG financing gap and declining official development assistance. In short, COVID-19 reversed some gains and slowed improvement across poverty, health, education, gender, and inequality, making 2030 targets much harder to reach.


Question: What are the challenges and opportunities in achieving the SDGs by 2030?

Answer: Challenges include a large financing gap (developing countries need roughly $4 trillion per year for the SDGs while facing about $1.4 trillion in debt servicing) and declining development aid; intensifying climate change and biodiversity loss undermining food security, health, and livelihoods; conflict and weak governance in fragile states; persistent inequalities and a digital divide (internet use remains around 35–39% in least developed and landlocked developing countries); and gaps in data and institutional capacity for planning and monitoring.

Opportunities include the falling cost of renewables and clean technology enabling alignment of energy, industry, and climate goals; digitalisation offering better access to education, health, and finance if connectivity and skills gaps are closed; expanded social protection (now covering over half the world’s population) as a base for resilience and poverty reduction; and proven local and partnership-driven initiatives (cities, businesses, civil society) that can deliver progress even when national progress is slow. Focusing on integrated “accelerator” packages (e.g. health and social protection, education and digital inclusion, clean energy, climate-resilient food systems) and targeting the most vulnerable regions and groups can maximise impact with limited time and resources.


Question: Your reflection and recommendations if any on SDGs progress

Answer: The SDGs remain essential as a shared framework, but COVID-19, climate change, geopolitical tensions, and underfinancing have made the full 2030 ambition unreachable for many targets. Rather than abandoning the goals, the focus should be on re-prioritising, accelerating the most transformative interventions, and making global cooperation fairer.

Recommendations: (1) Put SDG financing and debt reform at the centre of global negotiations—scale up concessional finance, rechannel special drawing rights, and link debt relief to climate and SDG investments. (2) Prioritise a small set of integrated “accelerator” packages per country (e.g. basic health and social protection, foundational learning and digital inclusion, clean energy access, climate-resilient food systems) that cut across many goals. (3) Target the most vulnerable regions and groups (e.g. Sub-Saharan Africa, fragile states, women and youth) with tailored investments and protection against shocks. (4) Strengthen accountability and data so that governments, businesses, and cities report transparently on SDG progress and move from pledges to measurable, time-bound action.

With these shifts, the world may not “achieve” all SDGs by 2030, but it can narrow the gap, avoid further reversals, and build a credible foundation for the decades after 2030.
